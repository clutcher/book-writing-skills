# Which writing rules transfer to cover copy

The `write-text` skill's rules assume a reader who has bought the book and reads it in order. Cover
and store copy are read by a stranger, in seconds, out of order, before buying. Read this table
before quoting any writing rule at the copy. Rules are named by what they say, not by number,
so the table survives a renumbering of the writing rules.

## Applies, and harder than in the manuscript

The reader has no earlier chapters to lean on, so anything not bridged is lost for good.

| Writing rule | Why it binds harder on a cover |
|---|---|
| Bridges and connectors: every sentence signals how it relates to the one before | A dangling `it` in a chapter is repaired by the chapter around it. On a cover nothing is around it. The *requirement* to connect binds harder; the manuscript's *method* of connecting (qualifiers appended at the end) does not, see below |
| One name per concept | The copy has room to name the central idea once. Naming it three ways in three sentences is the most common defect |
| Each sentence advances; no restatement | At cover length a restated sentence costs a claim the cover then cannot make |
| Claim strength matches the evidence | A cover cannot cite, so an overreaching claim has nothing holding it up, and a hostile reader tests it against the book |
| Plain (A2) vocabulary | The buyer is scanning, and many buyers in most fields read English as a second language |
| Agreement with the book's own definitions and thesis | A cover that misstates the thesis misprices the book for every reader who buys on it |
| Define terms before use | Holds in a narrower form, see the cover reading below |

## Suspended

These rules serve linear reading and get in the way of a cover.

| Writing rule | Why it is off |
|---|---|
| Pose the question before the answer | **Turned around.** The hook must BE the answer: a blurb that opens with a question and withholds the payoff is a blurb nobody finishes. What stays held back is the *proof*: the cover states the promise and shows the shape of the evidence, never the argument |
| Sentence length floor and target | Off. Short sentences are the main rhythm tool of copy, and a hook is usually under fifteen words. Judge by rhythm and by contrast with the sentences around it |
| Lists only for reference material | Off for benefit bullets. Scannable "after reading, you will" bullets are a category convention buyers look for |
| No bold in running text | Off. Bold is allowed; it works by stopping a skip (`emphasis-evidence.md`). How many bold spans a block should carry is a judgement, not a finding: nothing measures it. What is measured says "fewer" |
| Ground claims with references | Off, because copy carries no citations. This raises the bar on claim strength instead of lowering it |
| No forward references | Not applicable |
| Qualifiers appended at the end of a sentence (right-branching chains) | Off, and this one does the most damage when left on. Appending a qualifier to every sentence is how a chapter chains reasoning; at cover length it makes copy read as clunky and interruptive, because every qualifier is a stop and a reader who has not committed stops for good. Connect by sequence and short declarative sentences (cover rule C1). Too many paragraph breaks do the same damage from the other side: five blocks under thirty words in a row read as staccato |
| ASCII only | **Turned around.** Where the manuscript is kept ASCII-only, that is because it is build input. Copy is set type: curly quotes, real en dashes in ranges, a true ellipsis |

## Applies with a cover-specific reading

- **Define terms before use.** When the book coins a term, the cover may use it before
  defining it, because the unexplained word can be the hook. The narrower rule: no sentence's meaning may
  DEPEND on the coined word. Test by replacing the coined words with plain ones and
  re-reading; if a line collapses, it leans on a definition the reader does not have.
  The same test decides scope claims: if a claim "carries" a scope only through a coined
  word, a cold reader does not see that scope.
- **Pronouns.** `you` for the reader. Third person ("the author") for the author sentence: it is
  the category convention and keeps the page from reading as self-promotion. Never `we`.
- **Container nouns.** The copy genuinely needs one noun for what the book delivers. Whatever
  noun is chosen is used in exactly one sense on every surface. Check each candidate for a
  second sense in the same paragraph, not only for the count.
- **Voice.** The book's voice (as described in the author's voice file) still applies, but the
  copy's job is different: a claim and its proof shape, not an argument.

## Three rules the manuscript does not have

C1 one qualifier per sentence, C2 concrete subjects, C3 no repeated entry point along the
buyer's path. They are defined in `copy-review.md`.

## When a table entry and a writing rule disagree

The table wins for selling copy, and only for selling copy. If a new writing rule appears in the
write-text skill, decide once where it goes in this table (harder, suspended, cover reading) and
record the choice here, not in a review report.
