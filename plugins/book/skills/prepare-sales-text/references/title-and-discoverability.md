# Title, subtitle and discoverability

The title and subtitle are the one selling decision that cannot be changed after publishing.
This file holds the method for choosing them and the discoverability rules around them.

**Grade warning.** Almost everything here comes from author-service blogs and publishing
advice sites (WorldChangingBooks, WordsRated, ScribeMedia, Kindlepreneur, Reedsy and similar,
read in February 2026). Treat it as **OPINION or WEAK** unless a row names a stronger source. Platform
numbers (character limits, "ranking boosts", review thresholds, algorithm names) change and
several are folklore; verify each against the store's own help page (`store-limits.md`) before
relying on it. The one measured item: NYT bestseller titles have been getting shorter
(WordsRated, 3,444 titles, MODERATE).

## Seven rules of thumb

1. **Short main title.** Bestseller main titles run 2-5 words; the whole title and subtitle is
   scannable in about three seconds. Short titles travel: spine, thumbnail, conversation.
2. **Easy to spell, say and search.** Someone who heard it once at a conference must find it
   later. Avoid unusual spellings, homophones, and titles that match existing popular books.
3. **Main title is the hook, subtitle is the reel.** The title creates curiosity or states the
   promise; the subtitle says what the reader gets. A subtitle formula: audience, benefit,
   claim. Three-part subtitles are common.
4. **Emotional triggers.** Curiosity, transformation, pain avoidance, authority, simplicity,
   results. Readers of practical non-fiction respond to signals of usefulness, completeness, authority,
   transformation and pain relief. (OPINION; weigh against the book's own voice: a superlative
   undermines authority built on restraint.)
5. **One-word titles** are memorable and can become the name of a concept, but must be the
   perfect word, with no room to explain.
6. **Create a category, or join one.** Following the common pattern puts the book in a crowded
   namespace. A category-creating term is higher risk and higher reward: it becomes the
   reference name or is forgotten. A coined term needs a subtitle that carries the search words.
7. **Test before committing.** 3-5 serious candidates; ask target readers, not friends; search
   the stores and the web for conflicts; say it aloud; imagine it recommended at a conference;
   check it is spelled right after one hearing.

## Pattern templates

| Main title pattern | Examples of the shape |
|---|---|
| Adjective + noun | *Silent Spring*, *Deep Work* |
| The + adjective + noun | *The Intelligent Investor*, *The Selfish Gene* |
| Single word | *Sapiens*, *Salt* |
| Verb + noun | *Getting Things Done*, *Mastering the Art of French Cooking* |
| Noun + noun | *Food Rules* |
| A + noun + of + noun | *A Brief History of Time*, *A Short History of Nearly Everything* |

Subtitle shapes: "How to [result]", "The [adjective] guide to [topic]", "[Benefit], [benefit] and
[benefit]", "A [adjective] approach to [topic]", "Foundations of [topic]".

**Acronyms** work only when they spell a real word with a fitting meaning, each letter maps to a
memorable concept, and it can be pronounced. Forced letters weaken a title.

## Decision framework

Questions to answer before choosing: how much risk (safe pattern or new category)? What is the
primary signal (the idea, the evidence, the author's experience)? What does the subtitle do
(explain the thesis, promise a benefit, set authority)? Standalone book or possible series?

Checklist: main title 5 words or fewer; spellable after one hearing; creates curiosity or
promises a benefit; unique in store and web search; sounds natural when recommended aloud;
subtitle says what the reader gains; tested with target readers.

## Discoverability without an advertising budget

- **Title uniqueness check** on web search (exact title in quotes), each store, Goodreads, domain
  names, social handles and the places where the book's field shares its work.
- **The coffee-shop test:** a listener who hears the title once can find, remember and spell it.
- **A searchable word in the title or subtitle.** Without one the book lives on word of mouth.
  For a coined main title, the subtitle carries the search.
- **Thumbnail:** stores show covers at about 100-180 px; the title must read at that size (the
  `design-cover` skill checks this).
- **Amazon:** title plus subtitle under the store's combined limit; keywords that the title
  cannot hold go in the slots (`pick-keywords.md`); categories chosen from the live tree. Advice about
  an "A9/A10 algorithm", a "critical first 72 hours" and a "37% boost" for a keyword in the
  subtitle has no Amazon source; do not rely on it.
- **Also-bought placement** follows being bought together with other books; category choice
  decides which books a new title is grouped with.
- **Google Play Books:** no keyword boxes, so title, subtitle and description carry the search;
  categories are BISAC and more than one can be chosen; the full text is indexed and can surface
  in web search, which favours non-fiction. Keep a book on the platform once published; pulling
  and re-listing resets what the store has learned.
- **Across platforms:** main title short and unique; the subtitle carries the plain words a
  buyer would search, exactly as printed on the cover, and says the benefit. KDP rejects
  repeated generic keywords and promotional words ("bestselling", "free") in the title and
  subtitle fields (`store-limits.md`, Metadata Guidelines). Description with natural search
  words; title under about 60 characters so it shows whole in web results.

## What a non-fiction reader looks for

Lasting topics over passing trends; broad principles over details that date quickly; a clear benefit in the
title or subtitle; a professional cover; a strong table of contents, because the store shows it
in the sample, and one small study of students choosing library books suggests structure
decides the choice (`marketing-evidence.md`, purchase drivers, MODERATE).

## Record the decision

The chosen title, every rejected candidate with the reason, and any rejected acronym go to the
book's `decisions.md`, so a later review does not reopen a closed choice.
