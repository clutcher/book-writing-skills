# How publishers review non-fiction books

Background for choosing lenses and for knowing what a professional review would catch. This is a
summary of general practice across established non-fiction publishers, from training knowledge.
Processes change by publisher, field, book and year; treat the numbers as typical ranges, not rules.

## The usual shape

| What varies | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Review rounds | 1 to 4 |
| Developmental (structural) editing | from light to very strong |
| Expert readers per book | 0 to 4; academic and specialist presses often use more |
| Early readers | some publishers send chapters or a full draft to readers from the audience |

How established publishers stage review, in short:

- **A structural edit first:** the editor reads the whole draft for promise, order and audience
  before anyone looks at sentences.
- **Expert readers:** one or more specialists in the field read for accuracy and fairness. Some
  are picked from the target audience instead, to test whether the book works for them.
- **A fact-check or legal read** for books that name living people, give health, money or legal
  advice, or make claims a person could dispute.
- **Permissions:** quoted text, images, maps and tables taken from others are cleared before
  typesetting.
- **Developmental editing is kept apart from accuracy review:** structure and audience fit in
  one, facts and completeness in the other.

## The standard pipeline

1. Proposal to contract (1 to 3 months)
2. First draft (6 to 18 months)
3. Developmental edit (1 to 3 months, several passes)
4. Expert or accuracy review (1 to 2 months)
5. Revision after review (1 to 2 months)
6. Copy edit (2 to 4 weeks)
7. Author checks the copy edit (1 to 2 weeks)
8. Typesetting and layout (2 to 4 weeks)
9. Proof review (1 to 2 weeks)
10. Index (1 to 2 weeks, often with proofing)
11. Final proof (1 week)
12. Printing (2 to 4 weeks)

Total: about 12 to 24 months from contract to print.

## What expert readers say they check

- factual errors and outdated information;
- claims that go further than their sources;
- missing context: things the author assumes that readers will not know;
- gaps in explanations;
- passages that confuse even an expert (so the target reader has no chance);
- chapters that disagree with each other;
- other authors or schools of thought described unfairly;
- topics the audience will expect and not find.

What reviewers find most useful to give: honesty about what is confusing, not only about what is
wrong; reading as the target reader, not as an expert; noting every place they had to read twice;
checking that the order works (does chapter 5 need chapter 3?).

## What a developmental editor asks

1. Does the book keep its promise (subtitle, back cover, introduction)?
2. Is there a clear path from start to end?
3. Does each chapter earn its place?
4. Where will the reader get lost?
5. What could be cut with no loss?
6. Is the voice the same throughout?
7. Does the level fit the audience all the way?
8. Do the examples, stories and cases really show what they are there to show?

Things special to non-fiction: the balance of story and explanation (too many stories and the
point gets lost, too few and nothing sticks); a gradual, even rise in difficulty; repetition that
is planned teaching, not accident; examples real enough to believe and simple enough to follow.

## A combined review checklist

- **Accuracy:** facts, claims, figures and attributions correct; sources current; other views
  described fairly.
- **Completeness:** gaps in coverage; what the reader needs to know first is stated; limits and
  exceptions discussed.
- **Clarity:** clear writing; terms defined at first use; mark every passage read twice.
- **Order and flow:** chapter order; difficulty curve; few forward references; each section leads
  to the next.
- **Audience:** right level; fair assumptions about what readers know; useful to the target
  reader.
- **Examples:** relevant and realistic; the simplest example that works; enough context.
- **Engagement:** would you keep reading if you did not have to? Where does it drag?

An outside review with the default lenses covers all of these; the mapping is: accuracy → Lenses
2 and 3; completeness and order → Lenses 1 and 4; clarity and engagement → Lens 7; audience →
Lenses 4 and 6; examples → Lenses 2 and 4.

## When the book has code, recipes or other steps to follow

Some books print material the reader will run or follow: program code, recipes, exercises,
formulas, instructions. Publishers of such books add:

- **A separate proofreader for that material** after the text is complete: every listing,
  recipe or exercise is followed as printed.
- **Tools that pull the material out of the manuscript and test it** (for code: build and run
  it), so what is printed works.
- **A checklist line of its own:** the material is correct, follows as printed, and states its
  versions or quantities; the balance of material and text (too much of it is a reference manual,
  too little is hand-waving).
