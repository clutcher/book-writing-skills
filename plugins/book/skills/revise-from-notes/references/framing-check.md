# Framing check

Test the note's own idea before any option exists. Without this step, the options can all be
versions of one wrong idea, and the author has nothing to pick.

## The four questions

1. **Does the paragraph really have the problem the note assumes?** Find the real defect, not
   the surface complaint. A note that says "the cause is wrong" is often an **announcement**
   (`b`): the fix is to delete the announcement, not to rework the cause. A note that says
   "vague word" can be a sentence carrying two ideas (`d`).
2. **Is the note's frame the book's frame?** Reject general templates brought in from outside
   when they go against the book's own thesis. Examples of such ideas brought in from outside:
   - "more is always better", when the book argues that a little is enough (for example, more
     water in a garden bed that the book says to keep dry)
   - "X goes further than Y", when X and Y answer different questions (a false ladder)
   - a reason the book does not use (for example "because experts say so" in a history book that
     grounds each claim in the letters of the time)

   If the flagged sentence rests on such a frame, the fix is to change the frame, and every
   option that only "improves the sentence" is wrong.
3. **Does the claim match the book's own definitions?** Check a coined term against the chapter
   that defines it, the principle really at work, the category a pattern belongs to, and the
   precision of who said what (one person or the whole field; "similar" or "the same").
4. **Is every concrete claim true and defensible?** A number or finding the author could not
   defend is cut, not softened. Mark invented or unchecked numbers for removal, not rewording.

If the check changes the frame, say so in the **Framing check** field and build options on the
corrected frame only. Never hand the author three versions of a frame you doubt.

## Notes that attack the book's argument

Notes and review findings about the thesis often misread a deliberate set-up. A book often states
a problem strongly early on (two gardening guides that give opposite advice on when to sow, a
town history whose two main records disagree on the year of the flood) so that a later chapter
can resolve it.

1. Ask first: **is this a problem the book sets up on purpose and answers later?**
2. Find the chapter that answers it and read that part.
3. If it is a deliberate set-up, **decline the note**. Do not offer softer versions of the set-up:
   softening it removes the problem the later chapter solves.
4. Record it in `decisions.md` as `DECIDED / not a finding`, with the chapter that resolves it in
   `Why`, so no later review raises it again.

## Split notes

A note often holds two or more proposals. Split them and check each on its own. It is common for
one part to be already in the book (decline it and say where), one part to contradict the
chapter (decline it and say which sentence it contradicts), and one small part to be truly
missing (build options for that part only). Write under **What survives** which part is left.
