# Style lessons: truth and precision

Short lessons on how a sentence can read well and still be wrong or vague. Apply them in the write workflow (Step 5)
and in every review.

## Truth

- **Never attach a cause you cannot show to an observation.** An author can observe that a team
  delivers faster; the author cannot observe what happens in the readers' heads. State the
  observation, and give the mechanism only where the book grounds it.
- **Co-occurrence is not cause.** "The street is quieter, so fewer children are hurt" claims a
  cause; "the street is quieter, and fewer children are hurt" reports what was seen. Use the second unless the book shows the first.
- **A summary does not introduce a quantity the body never established.** A number, a ratio or a
  "most" in a summary must appear, with its grounding, in the chapter.
- **A summary leans on the chapter's definitions; it does not argue them again.**
- **Match the verb to the evidence.** "Shows", "proves", "means" state facts; "suggests" and
  "argues" state inferences. Do not strengthen a hedge to make a sentence plainer.
- **Keep the direction of cause and agency right.** People build habits, a change "leads to" a
  problem rather than "creates" it when a person is the one who acts; a tool the book says is
  the result must not be written as the source (Rule 12.3).
- **State a displaced position fairly** (`citing-in-prose.md`).

## Precision

- **Name every ellipsis.** When a sentence leaves something out, the reader must still know who
  acts, on what, from what, to what and when. "It moves the money" -> "The bank moves the money
  from the savings account into the current account on the first of each month."
- **Drop a redundant "from Chapter N"** when the named concept already points back to its home.
  Keep the chapter number only when the reader needs it to find the idea.
- **A qualified term keeps the scope the book gave it.** "True X" and "real X" narrow a term the
  book defined broadly (Rule 2.17).
- **Qualify a word that has a second strong sense in the field.** Write "mental model", not bare
  "model", in a book where "model" also means a statistical model or a scale model.
- **Avoid double negatives** ("neither ... nor ... not") in argument; state the positive.
- **Italicize a taught term at its formal definition**, not at an earlier preview mention
  (Rule 9.5).

## Cadence

- **Vary the shape.** A book whose every chapter opens with a recap, every section with a
  re-summary, and every sentence with a trailing "which means" reads as fatigue to outside
  readers, even when each sentence is fine (Rule 4.15 counterweight).
- **A book-wide style problem needs a named, countable checklist,** not another general sweep:
  "cut the section-opening recaps in these N sections, rewrite N chapter openings", with counts.
  A problem that survived a general copyedit will survive the next one.

## Hidden questions

A statement phrased as a question is only worth converting when the question and its answer are
adjacent sentences. A question whose answer arrives three paragraphs later leaves the reader
holding it open. Check the adjacency before turning a statement into a question or back.
