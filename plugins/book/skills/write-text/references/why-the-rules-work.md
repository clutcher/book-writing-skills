# Why the prose rules work

The prose rules rest on a few findings about how people read. This file gives the reasoning, so
a writer can apply a rule where its letter does not reach. The numbers in the rules (sentence
length, paragraph length, repetitions) are guidelines built on these findings; they are not
measured thresholds, and no study says "25 words" is where comprehension fails.

## The findings

- **A small working memory.** People hold about four items at once (Cowan 2001), not seven: the
  "seven plus or minus two" is a misreading of Miller (1956). Familiar patterns are compressed
  into one item (chunking), which is why a familiar term costs one slot and a new one costs
  several.
- **Readers predict.** The brain predicts the next word and the next idea, and an unexpected one
  costs extra processing. Headings, topic framing and consistent terms let the reader predict.
- **One embedded clause is fine; two is where it breaks.** In English, one level of
  self-embedding is no harder than none, and understanding fails at two (Miller and Isard 1964;
  Gibson 1998). This is about sentences, not about lists or tables.
- **Understanding builds one model at a time.** Readers build a mental model of the text and
  attach each new sentence to it; a sentence that fits no model is held loose and lost.
- **Cognitive load adds up.** Unexplained terms, open clauses and loose ideas all compete for the
  same small memory (cognitive load theory).

## From finding to rule

| Guideline | Why | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Keep sentences of moderate length | every open clause holds a memory slot until it closes | 3.6, 3.8, 4.3 |
| One idea per paragraph | the reader builds one model at a time | 3.3 |
| Few main ideas per section | about four items at once | 3.2, 3.7 |
| Clear structure: headings, explicit transitions | the reader predicts what comes next | 1.3, 2.2, 8 |
| Plain vocabulary | a familiar word is one item; an unfamiliar one is several | 5.4, 5.5 |
| Frame the question first | activates prediction before the details arrive | 1.3, 2.14 |
| Group visually (white space, headings) | the eye groups what is close | 9.3 |

"Lead with the main point" in journalism (the inverted pyramid) means the key facts come first
so a reader who stops early still gets them. A book chapter frames the **question** first and
lets the development earn the answer (Rule 1.3); it still never hides what the section is about.

## What this does not license

- No number here is a measured limit on sentence length or on how deep a list may go.
- Readability formulas do not measure understanding (`readability-scores.md`).
- What readers call readable and what they understand faster are not the same measure; ask
  readers to do something with the text, not only to rate it.
