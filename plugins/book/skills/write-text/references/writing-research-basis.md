# The research behind the writing rules

Where the structural rules come from: classical rhetoric, paragraph theory, cohesion research,
text structure and minimalism. Use it to explain a rule to the author or to judge a case the rule
does not cover.

## Classical rhetoric

Aristotle named three ways a text persuades: **logos** (the argument and its evidence),
**ethos** (the credibility of the writer, earned by expertise, character and goodwill) and
**pathos** (the reader's state and needs). Logic alone rarely persuades; a non-fiction book earns
ethos through careful argument and pathos by starting from the reader's problem.

The five canons are five separate skills: **invention** (finding the material), **arrangement**
(ordering it), **style** (choosing the words), **memory** (mastering the material) and
**delivery** (presenting it). Arrangement is its own skill and needs its own pass, which is why
the write workflow plans structure before drafting sentences.

## Paragraphs

- **Bain (1866)** set the textbook paragraph: unity (one topic), a topic sentence, coherence,
  and development.
- **Braddock (1974)** measured 25 essays from American magazines and found that only about 13%
  of paragraphs open with a simple topic sentence; unity often comes from an implied topic. Prescriptive rules and
  professional practice differ, so rule 1.3 asks for a *framed* topic, not a formula sentence.
- **Christensen (1963, 1965)** described cumulative sentences (a base clause plus modifiers that
  add detail) and paragraphs that move between levels of generality. It supports right-branching
  sentences (Rule 4.3).

## Cohesion and coherence

Halliday and Hasan (1976) named five devices that tie sentences together: **reference** ("it",
"this approach"), **substitution** ("the same", "the second one"), **ellipsis** (leaving out
what is recoverable), **conjunction** (and, but, so, then) and **lexical cohesion** (repeating
or relating words). Cohesion signals connections; readers build coherence from those signals and
context. Explicit signals lower the reader's effort, which grounds rules 2.2 and 4.2, and
consistent terms (Rule 2.4) are lexical cohesion.

## Text structure

- **Given before new.** A sentence is easier when it starts from something the reader already has
  and ends with the new information ("The bank keeps a record of every payment. This record is
  checked each month."). It grounds back-references at the start of sentences and fronted inversions that point
  back.
- **The inverted pyramid** (news writing) puts the most important facts first for readers who
  stop early. Reference text uses it; argued chapters frame the question first instead (Rule 1.3).

## Minimalism (Carroll)

Carroll's (1990) research on manuals found that readers do not read sequentially, that full coverage
overwhelms learners, and that people learn by doing real tasks. Minimalist documentation focuses
on the reader's tasks, keeps only essential information, gets the reader to real work quickly
and supports recovery from errors. For examples in a book: start with a worked example, make the
example task-oriented, show common errors, and cut everything unrelated to the lesson.

Reference material (looked up) and learning material (read through) need different shapes:
structured and complete for reference, minimal and task-led for learning. A book is mostly
learning material with reference parts (tables, checklists), which is why Rule 9.3 allows lists
only for reference and scanning.

If a paragraph needs a long footnote to be understood, ask first whether the paragraph could be
clearer.

## Working memory and sentence depth

About four items can be held at once (Cowan 2001, 2010). Center-embedding research puts the
break in understanding at two levels of self-embedding: one level is a little harder than none,
and recall drops sharply after that (Miller and Isard 1964; Gibson 1998). The limit is about English sentences only.
