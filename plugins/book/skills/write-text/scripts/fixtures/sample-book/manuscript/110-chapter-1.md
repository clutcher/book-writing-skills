# Chapter 1: What Rising Means

## 1.1 What A Dough Holds

Bread is baked far more often than it is planned, which means the cost of a rich plan falls on every loaf that comes out of the oven afterwards. Rivera argued that a cold kitchen slows the rise more than any change of flour [Riv19]. This matters.

This suggests something important about the way a dough rises through a long night without anyone checking on it again. The lean rise of a well-mixed dough lets a baker leave it alone, while *rich dough* forces the baker to go back. A good loaf has a single flavour, and each note of the crust should stay separate from the rest of the bread.

> **A float test** is a check that drops a spoon of starter into water to see whether it floats.

> **A poke test** is a quick press of the dough that shows whether it has risen enough.

## 1.2 What Lean Dough Means

The framework of this book rests on the two habits, and the Hartfield framework gives the home baker a fixed timetable in the same way. This chapter shows how a baker keeps the lean dough in mind while the rich leanness crust grows. Lean dough is easy because it is easy for anyone to shape and bake later.

The approach helps. From this observation emerges a rule that every later chapter applies to its own recipes in the same careful and patient way.
